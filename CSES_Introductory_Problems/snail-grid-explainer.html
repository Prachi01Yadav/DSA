A GRID PUZZLE, EXPLAINED FROM SCRATCH

The Snail Who Had To
Visit Every Square

Here's the whole problem in one breath: a snail starts in the top-left corner of a garden made of squares. It wants to reach the bottom-right corner. The rule is strict: it must step on every single square, exactly once. No repeats, no skipping. We want to know: how many different routes can the snail take?

One valid route on a 5×5 garden. Every square gets exactly one visit from the snail.

Plan A: Just Try Everything

This is what programmers call backtracking, and honestly, it's the least clever plan you could think of. It's basically: "when in doubt, try every door and see what happens."

Stand on a square. Look in all four directions: up, down, left, right. For every neighbor that isn't slimed yet, go slime it, and repeat this whole process from there. When you can't go any further, you either finished the garden or you didn't. Come back, wipe that last slime off, and try a different door.

Every time the snail "comes back and tries a different door," that's called backtracking. It's just undoing a move so you can try the next option.

Every full attempt like this is one possible route. We just add up how many of these attempts end successfully.

Bug Hunt: "Am I Actually Done?"

The tricky part of this whole puzzle isn't the moving around. It's knowing when to stop and declare victory (or defeat). Here's a very tempting first idea:

"If the snail has nowhere left to go, that means it's stuck. So I'll check: am I on the last square? If yes, count it. If no, throw it away."

Sounds reasonable. It's wrong, though — in two different ways.

The snail is squeezed into a corner with zero free neighbors. It's "stuck" — but three squares elsewhere never got visited.

Problem one: "stuck" only tells you about the squares right next to you. It says nothing about squares far away that never got touched. The snail above is 100% stuck, and also standing nowhere near the last square, and also the garden is nowhere close to finished. Your check needs to catch this too — but "stuck" alone doesn't tell the whole story.

The snail wandered onto the last square super early — plenty of squares are still bare.

Problem two, the sneakier one: what if the snail reaches the very last square, but way too early, with squares still unvisited elsewhere? It's not "stuck" at that moment — it might still have free neighbors to wander into. But it doesn't matter, because the last square is now slimed, and the snail can never step there again. The route is already ruined. Waiting until the snail gets fully stuck, several moves later, just wastes time discovering something you could've known immediately.

BUSTED

The Real Fix: Just Count Your Steps

Forget "stuck" entirely. Here's a much more direct idea: keep a running counter of how many squares have been slimed so far. Every time the snail moves to a new square, add one to the counter.

Now the only question you ever need to ask is: the moment the snail lands on the last square, does the counter equal the total number of squares in the garden?

if snail is on the last square:
    // did it slime EVERY square, not just some?
    if slimeCount == totalSquares:
        this route works! count it.
    else:
        too early — this route is dead. stop here.

Notice what disappeared: there's no "check if stuck" anywhere. If the snail truly has nowhere to go and isn't on the last square, the four-direction loop just quietly produces nothing on its own. You don't need a special case for it — it falls out naturally.

FIXED

TRICK #1Quit The Second You Cheat Early

Now for speed. The fix above already buys us our first real optimization for free: the moment the snail steps on the last square too early, stop right there. Don't keep exploring from that square at all — there's no point, since you can never revisit it to actually finish properly.

This sounds small, but it's a huge time-saver, because it stops a doomed route the instant it goes wrong, instead of only noticing many moves later.

TRICK #2Watch Out For Walls

Here's a neat pattern to notice: sometimes the snail bumps into a wall, and its only options are to turn left or turn right (it can't go straight, because straight is a wall too). When that happens, that turn can quietly slice the rest of the unvisited garden into two separate pockets.

Forced to turn at the wall — and now the leftover squares are split into two pockets that can't reach each other.

Since the snail is only one snail, it can only wander into one of those two pockets. Whichever pocket it picks, the squares sitting in the other pocket are stranded forever — there's no way back across to visit them.

The instant you notice this split, you already know the route is doomed. No need to keep crawling around for another twenty moves just to prove it.

The real book this idea comes from (the Competitive Programmer's Handbook) reports this single trick shrinking the search from 20 billion attempts down to 221 million — and the running time from 119 seconds down to 1.8 seconds. Same puzzle. Just noticing dead ends sooner.

TRICK #3Same Idea, Bigger Umbrella

Trick #2 only mentioned walls specifically. But actually, the wall doesn't matter at all — it's just one way to force a turn. The real, more general rule is:

Any time the snail is forced to turn (can't continue straight, but can turn left or right), check whether that turn splits the remaining garden into two disconnected pockets. If it does, this route is already dead — stop immediately.

This is the exact same trick as before, just not limiting itself to "only check this when we're touching a wall." With this wider net, the book reports the search finishing in just 0.6 seconds. Same 20-billion-attempt monster from the start, tamed down to something that finishes almost instantly.

BONUS TRICKThe "Check Everything" Version

Here's a trick that's less surgical than Trick #3, but easier to reason about: after every single move, do a quick flood — spread outward from where the snail is standing, only stepping on un-slimed squares, and count how many un-slimed squares you can actually reach.

If that number doesn't match how many un-slimed squares actually exist in the whole garden, something got cut off somewhere — dead end, stop now.

This catches every kind of split, not just the wall-and-turn kind. It's more thorough than Trick #3. The catch: it costs you a full sweep of the garden after every move, even on moves that were perfectly fine. More certainty, but more work per step. Whether that trade is worth it depends on how big your garden is.
